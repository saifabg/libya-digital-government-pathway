Two patterns matter for the narrative. First, Korea's rise in 2004 and its first places in 2010–2014 came through the **online services (OSI) and e-participation (EPI)** components; its telecommunications score was only 13th in 2010. Second, the slip after 2014 and the return to first in 2026 track the **human capital (HCI)** component much more than service delivery. Korea's OSI stayed in the top four throughout. The 2026 lead over Denmark is 0.0017. The brief warns that the 2024 TII revision limits comparability with earlier editions.

## (b) Turning points: mechanisms and limits

### 1. From 13th (2003) to the top five (2004–2008)

**Mechanisms.** The jump from 2003 to 2004 is almost entirely an online-services jump. OSI rose from 0.607 (19th) to 0.946 (4th), while TII and HCI hardly moved. This matches the delivery window of the Kim Dae-jung government's 11 priority projects. A presidential committee (SCeG, January 2001) cut across ministries. Its budgets were protected. Projects were time-boxed to finish by 2002. The G4C portal (1 November 2002) and e-procurement (September 2002) were live by the time the 2003–04 assessments were made [WB2016; Chung2015]. The 2001 e-government law gave a statutory purpose. Its 2003 amendment gave a legal basis for inter-agency data sharing [KLRI-EGA; OECD2025]. The 2003–07 roadmap's 31 projects then deepened the same back-end: administrative information sharing, Home Tax and Public Service 24 [WB2016]. Peer-reviewed accounts explain this period through:
- presidential leadership and policy continuity across administrations [Chung2022];
- a developmental-state legacy, democratisation and the shock of the Asian Financial Crisis, which together created urgency and capacity [Turner2022];
- a clear vision, strategic project priorities and focused resources [Chung2015].

Organisational re-engineering was not only technical. The Supreme Court Registry case shows that matching technology to business processes, building stakeholder trust and organisational learning were what made systems stick [KimPanPan2007].

**Limits.** These accounts are largely written by insiders or by scholars close to the programme, and they stress success factors. The coordination machinery was unstable: MOGAHA and MIC competed for control, and the Professional Committee of 2003–05 lacked legal status and was abolished [WB2016]. Chung and Kim argue that the e-government law itself became mainly about electronic documents rather than a budget-linked steering instrument [ChungKim2019]. Trust was contested. Citizen protests over the education information system and the planned e-ID card showed that efficiency-driven designs clashed with privacy norms [Jho2005]. The dip to 6th in 2008 (OSI down to 0.823) coincides with a change in UN scoring emphasis. We have not verified that link (see (e)), so it should not be read as a fall in performance.

### 2. First place, 2010–2014

**Mechanisms.** Korea reached OSI = 1.000 and EPI = 1.000 in 2010 and 2012, and EPI = 1.000 again in 2014. TII ranked only 13th in 2010. So first place was earned on what the UN measured as integrated, transactional services and participation, not on infrastructure. By 2010 the back-end built in 2003–07 was mature:
- 3,020 civil services could be requested online through Public Service 24 [WB2016];
- administrative information sharing had been extended and given its own legal basis (the World Bank lists a 2010 Act) [WB2016];
- KONEPS processed over 70% of procurement value [WB2016].

NIA, merged in 2009, acted as a single technical and project-management agency, running standards such as the 2010 e-government Standard Framework [NIA; WB2016]. Local e-government quality also mattered. Lim and Tang find that IT leadership and website quality drive decision performance in Korean cities, and that a "web divide" between cities produces uneven results [LimTang2008]. Use of e-government was associated with higher trust in government among Korean citizens, offsetting a negative association with general internet use [Im2014]. That is a plausible demand-side support for the participation scores.

**Limits.** The 2010–2014 results partly reflect a lag. They rest on investment and coordination built before 2008. In 2008 the Lee government abolished MIC and the innovation committee and spread ICT governance across bodies. NIA's own later account calls this a weakening of governance [WB2016; Hwang2023]. Top ranks therefore coexisted with weaker central steering, which suggests the index rewards accumulated assets rather than current governance quality. The ranks also rest on UN website assessment, which cannot see back-office quality, and Korean official sources never disaggregate the data enough to test individual mechanisms.

### 3. The slip to 2nd–4th (2016–2024)

**Mechanisms.** Korea's OSI stayed between 1st and 4th in every edition, and its TII between 2nd and 4th. The decisive drag was HCI, which ranked 18th–23rd from 2016 to 2024, against 6th in 2010–2014. Because the EGDI weights its three components equally, a mid-table HCI score cost Korea first place against Denmark, the UK and Australia. In 2024 Korea was tied first on OSI but fourth overall [UN-EKAP]. The main source of the HCI fall is likely a change in the HCI's education sub-indicators, but we have not verified this (see (e)). On the service side, reforms continued:
- **Government 3.0 (2013):** open data, life-event services and the Public Data Act [OECD2025];
- **GOV24 (2016–17):** consolidation of services on one portal [OECD2025];
- **the 2020 package:** "digital government" framing, the Data-Based Administration Act and the Intelligent Informatization Act [OECD2025];
- **2020 trust and inclusion reforms:** ending the public-certificate monopoly, which had been a long-standing usability barrier [KLRI-ESA; OECD2025], and the Digital Inclusion Action Plan [OECD2025].

**Limits.** Studies of Government 3.0 are critical. Nam argues that its "collective intelligence" logic was thin and participation was government-driven [Nam2016]. Park and Lee find that civil servants supported the innovation when well informed, but citizens' support did not rise with knowledge [ParkLee2015]. EPI fell to 9th in 2022. The OECD notes 670+ separate government apps and persistent practical barriers to data sharing. Tighter PIPA and RRN rules now restrict free cross-agency data matching unless a specific legal basis exists [OECD2025]. That is a direct trade-off between the once-only principle and privacy. These are reasons why services did not pull further ahead. They do not explain the rank movement, which is driven mainly by HCI.

### 4. Return to first place (2026)

**Mechanisms.** The 2022–2025 Digital Platform Government programme is the main reform of the period:
- integration of about 1,500 services and benefit notifications, MyData, and access through private channels [OECD2025; Hwang2023];
- the mobile ID card for all citizens (March 2025) [OECD2025];
- the relaunched GOV24+ (10 July 2025), with single sign-on and a Benefit Notification Service [MOIS-GOV24+].

These kept Korea's OSI at the ceiling (0.997). The EKAP data show, however, that the rank change itself came from HCI and TII. HCI rose from 0.912 to 0.947 (17th to 11th) and TII from 0.968 to 0.995, while OSI fell slightly, from first to fourth [UN-EKAP]. MOIS's own release credits "balanced progress" including human capital [MOIS-UN2026]. On the governance side, the 2023 and 2025 amendments to the E-Government Act strengthened central steering of local systems and incident resilience [OECD2025].

**Limits.** The margin over Denmark is 0.0017, and the gain comes from components (education statistics, telecom penetration) that digital-government reforms do not directly control. The 2026 return to first should therefore not be attributed to the GOV24+ relaunch or DPG without component-level evidence. A fire at the government's main data centre (NIRS, Daejeon) in late September 2025 was widely reported to have disrupted Government24 and hundreds of systems. We did not verify it from an official source (see (e)). If confirmed, it shows the concentration risk of a highly centralised shared infrastructure, and the 2025 resilience amendments came only shortly before it. Recent work characterises Korea as a "reactive platform-accountability" model, in which trust risks become visible mainly after major breaches [ParkMyeong2026]. The DPG committee's status after the 2025 change of government is also unclear (see (e)).

## (c) What is transferable to Libya, and what depends on conditions Libya lacks

**Plausibly transferable (low-cost design choices):**
1. **One mandated lead with protected money.** Korea's strongest delivery phase (2001–02) combined a presidential-level committee, a short list of time-boxed projects and budgets protected from cuts [WB2016]. Chung and Kim conclude that digital government works best when steered from, or tied to, the budget authority [ChungKim2019]. Legal mandates were useful, but a law focused on electronic documents was not enough on its own [ChungKim2019].
2. **Sequence the shared backbone before front-end portals.** That means a unique person identifier, a legal basis for inter-agency data sharing, and a single portal. Korea legislated data sharing (2003 amendment) alongside the portal and then spent 2003–07 on back-end integration [KLRI-EGA; OECD2025; WB2016].
3. **A dedicated technical agency** (NIA-type) for standards, project oversight and supervision of contractors, separate from the policy ministry [NIA; WB2016].
4. **Workforce and process change built into each project:** training cohorts and train-the-trainer roll-outs [WB2016], and process alignment rather than plain digitisation [KimPanPan2007].
5. **Inclusion and measurement from the start:** assisted channels and digital-skills centres [OECD2025], plus a recurring usage and satisfaction survey [OECD2025].
6. **Lessons from Korea's mistakes:** avoid locking citizens into one mandated authentication technology (Korea needed until 2020 to undo the 1999 certificate monopoly [KLRI-ESA; OECD2025]); consult on privacy before rolling out national databases [Jho2005]; build disaster recovery into shared data centres.

**Dependent on conditions Libya lacks or must first establish:**
- **A unified, stable executive** that can carry one programme across administrations. Korean success is attributed to continuity across presidencies [Chung2022] and a developmental-state legacy [Turner2022], and even Korea's coordination broke down in 2003–05 and after 2008 [WB2016].
- **A capable, career bureaucracy and an effective policy process**, which Turner and colleagues list as preconditions. They state explicitly that the whole Korean model is not replicable [Turner2022].
- **Decades of prior digitisation**, including citizen registration databases computerised since the 1980s [WB2016], and a nationwide broadband build-out from 1995 [OECD2025].
- **Fiscal capacity and a domestic ICT industry**: the IPF from 1993 and private authentication and cloud vendors [WB2016; OECD2025].
- **Crisis-driven consensus**: the 1997 crisis accelerated reform [Turner2022; OECD2025]. A crisis in a fragmented polity may have the opposite effect.

Comparative work using the design–reality gap framework (Malaysia versus Korea) points the same way: transfer depends on closing context-specific gaps, not on copying systems [Ramli2017]. Any claim about Libya's own identity, registry or connectivity starting point should come from the paper's Libya evidence. This file does not assess it.

## (e) Not verified

1. **Enactment date of the 2001 e-government law (28 March 2001).** Seen only in search-result snippets. The loaded KLRI page confirms Act No. 6439 and entry into force on 1 July 2001; the OECD says "enacted in July 2001".
2. **Entry into force of the 2020 Electronic Signature Act on 10 December 2020.** Derived from "six months after promulgation" (promulgated 9 June 2020). The Library of Congress page that states the date directly returned HTTP 403.
3. **RRN introduced in 1968.** KLRI confirms an amendment (Act No. 2016, 29 May 1968), but the loaded version shows later wording, so the content of the 1968 amendment was not seen.
4. **Date and status of the Presidential Committee on Digital Platform Government.** The 2 September 2022 launch appears only in news and WEF pages. The OECD says "established in 2023"; the 2023 NIA presentation places DPG from 2022. dpg.go.kr did not resolve (DNS failure) on 22 November 2026, and the committee's status after the 2025 change of government is unknown.
5. **Exact launch date of the "Government24" brand.** The OECD says consolidation under GOV24 began in 2016; no official page giving the launch date was loaded.
6. **Administrative Information Sharing Center launch date and the exact name and number of the 2010 sharing law.** Only the World Bank's table entry, "Act on Shared Utilization of Public Administration Information (2010)", was seen; it was not checked on KLRI.
7. **Date of the 31-project roadmap (August 2003).** Seen only in search snippets. A KDI K-Developedia split of the 31 projects (18/13 versus 10/21) is internally inconsistent and was not used.
8. **Establishment of the National Computing and Information Agency / Government Integrated Data Center in 2005.** Supported only by a Korean-language journal abstract and an indirect World Bank mention; not used in the timeline.
9. **Fire at the NIRS data centre, Daejeon (26–27 September 2025), and outage figures (647 or 709 systems; Government24 down).** News only; no official MOIS or NIRS page was loaded. Whether it affected the 2026 UN assessment window is unknown.
10. **Why HCI ranks shifted (2014→2016 fall; 2024→2026 rise) and why OSI dipped in 2008.** These may reflect UN methodology or data-source revisions rather than real change. The UN survey methodology annexes were not consulted. The UN DESA 2026 press release (un.org) returned HTTP 403.
11. **Sub-index ranks reported in the media for 2026 (OSI 4th, TII 5th, HCI 11th).** Consistent with our recomputation from EKAP data, but the MOIS attachment containing the official figures was not opened.
12. **Gestión y Política Pública article** (Kim & Chung, 2016; OpenAlex W2547101434). Its DOI 10.29265/gypp.v25i2.201 is not registered with Crossref; the article was not used.
