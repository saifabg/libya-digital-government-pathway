## (b) Turning points

### 1. 2003–2008: from 140th to 79th

**Mechanisms.** The jump followed a period of building institutions. The IDSC (1985) and MCIT (1999) had created a central ICT capacity unusual for the region (Kamel, 2021). Early in 2004 the e-government programme moved to MSAD, which tied it to administrative reform rather than to telecoms policy (Hassan & Abdelkader, 2011). MSAD ran flagship e-services such as university enrolment and citizen relationship management. Interview evidence credits the CRM project's success mainly to "changing the organisational culture and thinking" (Hassan & Abdelkader, 2011). MSAD also worked on G2G service development and interoperability governance (Klischewski & Askar, 2012). Its minister presented e-government as the tool of a wider "good governance" programme (Darwish, 2008). For surveys that weighted portal presence and online information, a national portal and a set of visible services were enough for a large rank gain.

**Limits.** The 2001 start and the January 2004 portal launch are supported only by secondary sources; the official portal page could not be loaded. Darwish (2008) is a minister's keynote, not independent evidence. The gains were driven from above, and the case studies describe programme-wide problems (Hassan & Abdelkader, 2011). They do not show broad adoption by citizens.

### 2. 2010–2022: fluctuation and stagnation (79th–114th)

**Mechanisms.** The rank swung between 79th and 114th without a lasting trend. The evidence points to front-end services that were not backed by back-office integration. By 2021 the World Bank found that core systems remained "only partially operational and are not inter-operable". Human-resources systems were "separate for each government entity", the financial management system was not linked to procurement, and online services still relied on paper processes behind the scenes (WB2021, pp. 22–23). Project-level studies show early successes relapsing (ElKadi, 2013). Ministry websites used social media almost entirely to publish information, with little two-way interaction (Abdelsalam et al., 2013). Staff fears of losing control and of extra workload measurably reduced the efficiency and effectiveness of e-government systems (Elgohary & Abdelazyz, 2020). The 2011 political transition and changes of government in the following years also interrupted continuity. We found no peer-reviewed source that documents what happened to MSAD's e-government mandate after 2011.

**Limits.** Part of the swings, such as 107th in 2012 and 79th in 2014, may reflect changes in the survey method and in other countries' progress rather than real changes in Egypt. We could not attribute them to specific Egyptian events.

### 3. 2020–2026: Digital Egypt, 95th in 2024 and 109th in 2026

**Mechanisms.** From 2019 MCIT led the programme as "Digital Egypt", aligned with Egypt Vision 2030 (MCIT-DE). It started with a full-service pilot in one governorate (Port Said, 2019), then national fibre connection of state buildings, and a national platform from 2020, officially launched in July 2022 (MCIT2020; MCIT2023). Ministries moved to the New Administrative Capital from 2023, after mass digital-skills training of relocating staff (MCIT2023; MPED2023). This was an organisational shock that forced paper-to-digital conversion. A data-protection law (2020), a coordination fund attached to the Prime Minister's office (2021) and Cloud First and Open Data policies completed the governance package (WB2021; MCIT-DE). Egypt's 2020 EGDI score had already moved it from the "middle" to the "high" group (WB2021). By 2024 the rank had reached 95th.

**Limits.** Delivery fell well short of targets. The platform offered about 45 services in February 2021 against a target of 550 by 2023 (MCIT2021), and just over 165 at its official launch in 2022 (MCIT2023). A remote digital ID was still only announced as "coming soon" in November 2025 (SIS2025). Privacy and security concerns about the expansion of digital services have been raised (Sherif & Labib, 2025), and we could not verify that the law's executive regulations are in force. The fall to 109th in 2026 probably reflects other countries advancing faster and the effects of the TII revision. This is an inference; we have not checked it against the 2026 component scores.

## (c) What is transferable to Libya, and what depends on conditions Libya lacks

**Transferable.**
- A single-governorate "full-service" pilot (Port Said) before national rollout. This lowers risk and produces evidence.
- Connecting state buildings by fibre as a prerequisite programme with its own budget line.
- Pairing a front-end platform with a deliberate back-office plan. Egypt's experience is a warning: a portal without interoperable HR, finance and procurement systems stalls (WB2021).
- Building in change management for staff from the start, given measured resistance (Elgohary & Abdelazyz, 2020).
- Passing data-protection legislation early, and making sure its regulator and regulations follow quickly.

**Dependent on conditions Libya lacks.**
- A strong, unified central executive able to enforce decisions across ministries, including moving whole ministries to a new capital (MPED2023).
- Stable, long-lived central institutions (IDSC since 1985, MCIT since 1999).
- The fiscal capacity for very large projects (EGP 6 bn fibre programme; a new administrative capital).
- A state telecoms operator able to build national data and fibre infrastructure.

Egypt also shows that high-level political backing produces rank gains that do not last. Without stable back-office integration and organisational change, Libya should not expect a portal-led strategy to hold its gains.

## (e) Not verified

- **2001 start of the e-government programme inside MCIT, and the transfer to MSAD.** The 2001 start and the alternative year (2003 vs. 2004) for the transfer rest on secondary sources found by search. Only "MSAD responsible since early 2004" comes from a verified peer-reviewed abstract.
- **January 2004 launch of www.egypt.gov.eg.** The official "About" page returned an expired TLS certificate on 22 Nov 2026 and was not loaded; we did not bypass the warning.
- **Personal Data Protection Law No. 151 of 2020.** The number, the Official Gazette issue 28 (bis) E of 15 July 2020 and entry into force in October 2020 come only from law-firm and publisher copies (Lexis Middle East, ACC, Andersen). MCIT confirms that a Personal Data Protection Law exists but gives no number. Executive Regulations (Ministerial Decree 816 of 2025) are not verified.
- **Numbers and dates of other laws** (Electronic Signature Law; Anti-Cybercrime Law) were not checked. They are not given in the file.
- **National ID database.** We found no official or peer-reviewed source on when the Civil Status national-ID database was created or how it links to Digital Egypt.
- **Digital Identity app.** Only an announcement ("My Digital ID ... will be introduced soon", Nov. 2025) was verified. No launch was confirmed.
- **Government data centres.** We could not confirm, from an official source, a government data centre in the New Administrative Capital or a full list of centres. MCIT headlines mention plans for an integrated data centre and a national cloud strategy (2026), but these were not read in full.
- **Inconsistent counts and dates for the Digital Egypt pilot.** MCIT gives 70 services in Aug. 2020 and 45 in Feb. 2021; the World Bank dates the pilot to July 2020, while MCIT's 26 Aug. 2020 release says August.
- **Egypt Vision 2030.** The launch year (2016) was not checked against an official source.
- **Causes of the 2012 and 2016–2018 drops and the 2026 fall.** No source we verified links these to specific Egyptian events.
