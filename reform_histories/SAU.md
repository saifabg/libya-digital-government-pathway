# Saudi Arabia (SAU): digital-government reform history, 2000–2026

**Access note.** On 22 Nov 2026 the live official sites dga.gov.sa, vision2030.gov.sa, sdaia.gov.sa and laws.boe.gov.sa refused automated access: HTTP 403, a Cloudflare "you have been blocked" page, or a timeout. yesser.gov.sa no longer resolves. ## (b) Turning points

### 1. Slow climb, 2003–2014 (104th → 36th)

**Mechanisms.** The climb follows a classic programme-office model. The 2003 royal decrees put money (Finance) and a sector mandate (MCIT) behind e-government. From 2005 Yesser was a joint MCIT/Finance/CITC programme. It was not a ministry, and its philosophy was to "reduce centralization… while ensuring the minimum level of coordination" (Y1). It worked on three fronts:
- **Shared infrastructure** that agencies could not build alone: GSN, GSB, data centre, SSO, portal, e-payment link, and the smart national ID as an e-government pillar (Y1, Y2).
- **A legal base**: the 2006 implementing regulations and the 2007 Electronic Transactions Law (Y3, L1).
- **Organisational levers inside agencies**: CIO-level posts (2007), mandatory e-government committees chaired by agency heads, and a duty to measure progress, with Yesser reporting to the King (Res. 252, 2010; Y3, Y4).

The rank gains match this sequence. OSI rose from 0.18 to 0.80 between 2003 and 2012 (rank 100 → 18) and drove the rise, while HCI ranked low throughout. Vision 2030 reads the period the same way: it credits online employment, passports and civil affairs, payments and commercial registration services for the climb from 90 (2004) to 36 (2014) (V1). Alzahrani (2022) revisited government websites surveyed in 2006–2012 and found "remarkable improvements" in their maturity, and argues that investment and service improvement reinforce each other.

**Limits.** Peer-reviewed studies from this period describe a supply-led push that ran ahead of use:
- Al-Fakhri et al. (2008) found most agency websites "ineffective". They called for awareness, wider internet access, a legal framework for e-transactions, staff IT training and electronic fund transfer.
- Adoption studies point to perceived complexity, privacy and internet quality as barriers (Al-Ghaith et al., 2010), and to gaps in the quality of service procedures (Alanezi et al., 2012).
- Payment infrastructure and the digital divide were weak enough that private intermediaries in Madinah filled the gap (Al-Sobhi et al., 2010; Weerakkody et al., 2013).

The EGDI climb therefore reflects mainly the online offer and infrastructure, not take-up.

### 2. Dip to 44th (2016) and 52nd (2018)

**What the data show.** The level did not collapse. EGDI was 0.690 in 2014, 0.682 in 2016 and 0.712 in 2018. Other countries simply moved faster.
- 2016: OSI fell from 0.77 to 0.67 (rank 17 → 48).
- 2018: OSI recovered to 0.79, but its rank stayed at 49. TII fell from 0.57 to 0.53 (rank 47 → 67) and EPI fell from rank 40 to 67.

**Plausible mechanisms.**
- **A gap between strategies.** The Second Action Plan ran to 2016 and the Third covers 2020–2024 (Y7). Vision 2030 and the NTP (2016) shifted strategic authority to the Vision Realization Programs while Yesser stayed a programme office inside MCIT. In that window the portal and service offer were being redesigned rather than expanded. This is inferred from the timing; no source states it.
- **Survey methodology.** OSI and TII are relative scores and their indicators were revised in 2016 and 2018, which moves ranks even when national services are unchanged.
- **Unresolved implementation barriers.** Makki and Alqahtani (2022) model institutional habits and weak political coordination as the root barriers to digital government. Zygiaris and Maamari (2023) argue that the move from e-government to digital government needed organisational change, open government and social inclusion, not just technology.

**Limits.** No source I could verify explains the 2016–2018 dip directly. The account above is an inference from the timing and the sub-indices.

### 3. Jump from 31st (2022) to 6th (2024) and 4th (2026)

**Mechanisms.**
- **A target written into national strategy.** Vision 2030 named a top-five UN E-Government rank as a goal (V1). The 2026 result (4th) meets it. Because the index was itself the target, agencies were steered towards the features the UN Survey assesses.
- **A single regulator with enforcement and measurement powers.** The DGA (2021) reports to the top of government. It issues binding policies, including once-only, digital by default and whole-of-government platforms (D3, D4). Qiyas became a compliance audit against 111 standards, explicitly aimed partly at international indicators (D5). The DXMI ranks platforms on satisfaction and user experience (D6).
- **Consolidated front-end and identity layers on infrastructure Yesser had already built.** These are the GOV.SA unified platform with single digital identity (Y9), Unified National Access/Nafath run by the NIC (N1), the GSB with 152 linked agencies (Y10), and Absher, which a peer-reviewed study puts at 23 million authenticated users (Alluhidan et al., 2022).
- **Sub-index movements.** OSI went from 0.82 to 0.99 (rank 32 → 4) and EPI from rank 43 to 7, both the parts most sensitive to portal consolidation and the UN Member States Questionnaire, which Saudi Arabia returned in 2024 (U1). TII went from 0.87 to 0.96 (and 1.00 in 2026) under the revised 2024 TII.
- **Leadership, as the UN reads it.** The UN Survey 2024 attributes top ranks partly to "strong digital leadership" and names Saudi Arabia (U2).

**Limits.**
- Part of the 2024 jump reflects the revised TII.
- OSI and EPI measure what is offered online and reported in the questionnaire, not outcomes. HCI rank stayed at 19.
- Case studies inside agencies still report legacy-integration, interoperability, financing and resistance problems (Alshahrani, 2025), plus data-sharing, governance and language/culture tensions in AI adoption (Alshahrani et al., 2022).
- Accessibility audits found that mobile government sites did not comply with WCAG (Al-Sakran & Alsudairi, 2021).
- Gains in inclusion depended on wider social reforms, not only on technology. Women's independent use of m-government for passports and driving licences followed the lifting of the guardian-permission requirement, and religious, cultural and connectivity barriers remain (Alotaibi et al., 2025).
- Trust does not translate simply into perceived quality (Santa et al., 2019).

## (c) What transfers to Libya, and what depends on conditions Libya lacks

**Transferable, at low cost and in sequence:**
1. One accountable lead body with a legal mandate to set standards, measure agencies and report to the head of government. This can start as a programme office, Yesser-style, before becoming a regulator, DGA-style.
2. Shared building blocks built once and offered free to agencies: a secure government network, a service bus or data exchange, single sign-on tied to the national ID number, and one portal. Yesser even paid agencies' connection costs.
3. An early legal base: an e-transactions/e-signature law, implementing regulations, and later a data protection law with a long transition period.
4. Agency-level levers: a CIO post and an e-government committee chaired by the agency head in every ministry, plus a published yearly compliance measurement.
5. Assisted channels (intermediaries, a contact centre) while connectivity and digital skills are low.

**Depends on conditions Libya lacks:**
- One executive able to enforce standards across all ministries. In Saudi Arabia, binding resolutions came from the Council of Ministers and the DGA reports to the Prime Minister.
- Twenty years of steady funding. Saudi Arabia ran three successive action plans and kept the same institution throughout.
- A single civil registry and national ID run by one interior-ministry data centre (the NIC).
- Near-universal connectivity (TII 1.00 in 2026) and a large domestic ICT services sector.
- Political stability long enough to move from building infrastructure to consolidating platforms.

Saudi Arabia's index-led and highly centralised model also has costs that Libya should weigh: incentives to optimise for the index, uneven usability and accessibility, and trust and privacy questions in a centralised identity system.

## (e) Not verified

- **Absher**: launch year (often said to be about 2011–2015). Only its existence, its three portals and 23 million users (Alluhidan et al., 2022) are verified.
- **Nafath**: launch date. Secondary sources say it was announced 28 Nov 2016 by MoI; the 2021 app is attributed to SDAIA/NIC. Only that it was live by Aug 2019 is verified.
- **my.gov.sa / GOV.SA**: launch date of the unified platform. Only that it existed by 2020–2022 is verified (Y9). my.gov.sa itself was not accessed.
- **Vision 2030**: exact approval date (commonly 25 April 2016). Only "2016" is verified.
- **Digital Government Policy**: exact issue date. A search snippet says 29 Sep 2021; the official page could not be loaded.
- **Dates and sequence**: Gregorian equivalents of Hijri dates other than those stated by the BOE; start year of the first Yesser measurement round; content of the Third Action Plan (2020–2024).
- **Institutional changes**: whether and when Yesser was formally merged into the DGA. Amer and GOV.SA appear under the DGA by 2023–2025, but no merger instrument was found.
- **DGA claims**: 3rd in the World Bank GovTech Maturity Index 2022, and ESCWA GEMS ranks. These are reported only by the DGA and were not checked against World Bank or ESCWA documents.
